Figuring out what is going on

<< Slipping attendance

As soon as your child shows signs of difficulty with school attendance, investigate what’s going on for them and around them, to understand their reasons for not attending. This can help you to know the types of support they need and identify your next steps.

Thinking about my child

Think about possible reasons that may cause distress and prevent your child from going to school. These can be situations or events that are new or ongoing, and happen to them or around them.

Consider if there are:

  • Barriers to learning or participation. Challenges in the classroom or during activities such as following spoken instructions, handwriting, noise, inaccessible equipment or school grounds, feeling anxious and managing frustration.
  • Academic concerns, such as keeping up with school work, taking tests, talking in front of the class and completing homework.
  • Friendship and peer concerns, such as no one to spend lunchtime with, group exclusions, feeling isolated and bullying.
  • Changes at home, such as having an ill family member, moving house or parents feeling highly stressed.
  • School-related concerns including attending a new school or year level, having new/replacement teachers and classmates or managing routine changes (e.g. camps, excursions, sports day).

All of these can affect how young people think or feel about school and themselves.

They may have other reasons for avoiding school. Read about causes of school attendance challenges.

My child’s thoughts and feelings about school

Whether the initial reasons are clear to you or not, it is best to talk with your child and hear directly from them. This may help you to confirm what you already know, identify what else might be affecting them and work out the exact underlying cause or the most important one (if there are multiple causes).

Before starting a conversation about school,

  • be familiar with the key knowledge of working with your child
  • ensure that all caregivers are working towards the same school attendance goals.

Begin with your observations

Start the conversation broadly and gently. Tell your child that you noticed something is not quite right by saying something like:

  • "I noticed that you don't seem so happy going to school at the moment."
  • "I noticed that you have been feeling sick a lot lately."
  • "You have gone a bit quiet lately. I wonder if there is something going on for you."

Listen carefully

Encourage your child to express whatever they are feeling. Listen deeply to them, so that they feel heard, seen and understood. This will help you to fully understand their experiences and provide the comfort they need to navigate a difficult period. Listen to your child

<++> How you might show active listening
Be fully present. Ensure there is plenty of time, privacy and no distractions to talk. For example, in the evenings or at weekends, but not just before school. Let other family members know that you will attend to them later.
Observe non-verbal cues. Does your child’s body language appear open and relaxed, or constricted and closed off?
For example, they may be nervous or anxious if they are biting their nails, trembling or talking too fast. If they talk slowly, they may be tired or trying to carefully choose their words.
Relax. Use open, non-threatening body language (e.g. not folding your arms, leaning in) and facial expressions (e.g. smiling while listening, nodding at key points).
Ask open-ended questions. Ask questions about school such as:
- “What are your favourite / least favourite parts of school?”
- “How are you managing with the school work?”
- "What have you been doing at lunch time?"
- "Sounds like everything sucks. Is there something that does not suck as much?"

You could also ask imaginary questions about school. Your child’s answers may reveal challenges that they are experiencing.
- "What does a perfect school look like for you?"
- "If you could change one/three thing(s) about school, what would it be?"

The more you know about the types of situations or challenges that affect your child, the more informed you are to make decisions about how best to support them.
Reflect back. You might say:
- “It sounds like you don’t want to go to school because…”
- “I think I understand why you find it hard to go to school. It’s because…”
- “It seems like you’re really struggling to figure out what’s going on for you at the moment.”
Stay neutral Avoid showing judgement, including shame, blame or criticism, even if you disagree. Instead, be curious about their experiences of school, including how they felt, what happened as those feelings occurred, and what they did next.

- "When do these feelings happen?"
- "What was going on?"
- "What thoughts did you have?"
- "How did you manage that?"
be patient Reliving difficult experiences can be distressing for everyone. Give your child time to think or calm down. Assure them that it is ok to rest and encourage them to try again when they are able to.
- Doing something, such as drawing or taking a walk together, while discussing tough feelings may help them feel comfortable.

be patient

They may feel distressed and find it hard to respond to direct questions about what’s going on. They may not be able to identify exactly what their feelings are or why they are feeling them. They may not know the reasons for their school attendance challenges or may not be willing to talk about them.

Not getting a response?

Remember to take time to talk about things other than school, when getting to school is tricky.

  • Reassure them that you care, you know something is wrong and want to understand so you can help them
  • Suggest other ways of communicating with you. For example, say "send me a text" or "write me a note".
  • Remind them that they can approach you when they are ready to talk.
  • Let them know that you will check-in again. This shows your child that you care and they are important to you.

Still not getting a response?

If your child won't open up to you after repeated tries and you are concerned about what is going on, let them know that you are going to seek help. Add statement to avoid blaming child. Before doing so, you may wish to explore and agree with other caregivers on your next steps.

  • Speak to school staff.
  • See your GP for further advice. They can check if your child's physical and mental health explains their school attendance challenges.
  • Talk to an adult who knows your child well. They might be grandparents, other family members, a family friend or a parent of your child's friend.

If you booked an appointment to see the GP or speak to the school, communicate this with your child.

Other considerations

  • Speak positively about education and help your child to think and talk about what they like about school life (e.g. friends, PE, art, lunchtime), though it may be challenging for children to recognise any positives at this point. Despite their school attendance challenges, focus on maintaining your child's interests in school and most importantly, learning.
  • Record your observations. This includes the signs of school attendance challenges and when they occur, the days your child attends and doesn't attend school and the strategies that have been trialled.
  • You can keep an attendance diary to gather these information, track progress and identify any missing school patterns. It can also help you to explain the situation to school staff or other professionals.

Getting in touch with the school

You may or may not be able to figure out what your child needs straightaway.

After talking to your child, it is recommended that you speak to their school about your concerns and what you already know about your child's attendance challenges. They can help you to identify and address the causes. Find out how to raise a concern and seek support from the school.

You may feel let down by your child's school or angry about their experiences. Prioritising your own wellbeing will help you to better support your child through this challenging time. Looking after yourself

Address the causes

After talking to young people and listening to their concerns about going to school, you may have ideas on what is going on for them and their reasons for avoiding school. Seek their input on where you might provide support or how they would like the problem resolved, to support their attendance. Supporting school attendance

Sometimes, additional and professional supports may be needed to help young people address other problems in their life, before they can attend school. seek-additional-supports
