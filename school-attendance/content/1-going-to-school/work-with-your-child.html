Working with your child

< Going to school

Working well with your child helps families to run smoothly, work as a team and reduce conflict. It is built on knowing your child and having a strong and supportive relationship with them.

Why is it important?

When you work with your child, according to their preferences and individual circumstances, it:

  • makes it easier for you to solve problems and meet their needs, and this supports their development and wellbeing
  • helps young people feel secure, which gives them confidence to try new things and that you can support them
  • reminds everyone that they are on the same team and working together toward common goals.

How to work well together

Here are things you can do to better understand your child and work well together.

Communicate often

Build a routine of talking about school and other things in your child’s life.

  • What? Be curious about their experiences and relationships in school, including their strengths, challenges and any specific goals they are working toward, as well as their interests and hobbies.
  • When? Set aside a consistent time and place for catching up regularly, in a relaxed environment when everyone is calm and can think clearly. For example, car travel or when doing the dishes.
  • How? Use flexible communication methods. Talking, drawing, writing, typing, voice notes or even memes may help young people to express themselves.

Create safety

Create a safe space for sharing, where your child can trust you and feel comfortable being vulnerable around you, and this strengthens your relationships.

Feeling secure gives them confidence to express their thoughts and feelings openly and honestly, like they are being heard, seen, understood and accepted.

How to be a safe space

You can help your child feel secure around you by fully listening and respecting all views even if they differ from yours. To be a better listener, learn how to use active listening.

Show your child that you genuinely care and want to support them by validating their feelings and offering them comfort in the way that makes them feel safe and supported. Expressing your empathy

Check in with your emotions

Our emotions can affect the way we communicate and respond to others, so it’s important to pay attention to them as they come up.

Here are some situations where our emotions matter, and what you can do, to work well with your child:

  • Observe non-verbal messages. These are our body language and facial expressions. For example, hugs, a smile, eye contact and stopping what you are doing show that you want to be close or are ready to listen to your child. But a grumpy tone of voice, crossed arms, a frown or turning your body away may show that you don’t want to be there.
  • Show mutual respect. It is common for tensions to arise with your child during disagreements. They may also desire not to talk, especially as they move towards the teenage years. It is about respecting opinions and wishes, finding constructive solutions, and working towards compromises and common goals.
  • Be patient and persistent. It may take time and several tries before your child opens up to you. If they are not ready, inform them that you will make another time to check-in with them.
  • Be open to discussing difficult things. Things like mistakes, alcohol, risky behaviours, romantic relationships and all kinds of feelings (e.g. anger, joy, jealousy, frustration, fear, anxiety, disappointment). Depending on your child’s experiences or your values, these discussions might cause strong emotions. If possible, begin only when you are calm, or plan ahead what to say.

When is it needed

It is important to keep communication open and build a positive, supportive relationship with your child, even when things are going well. This helps you to support them when things are not going well and you need to figure out what’s going on.

When you work well with your child, you also make it easier for them to attend and participate in school and activities. Other ways you might help with maintaining regular attendance.
