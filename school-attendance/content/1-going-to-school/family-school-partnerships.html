Family-school partnerships

< Going to school

Family-school partnerships are valuable for all students but become critical when attendance and engagement challenges emerge. Develop strong, positive relationships with the school by establishing a partnership from the beginning and keeping communication open throughout, even when things are going well.Raising a concern or seeking supports are some situations where you might need to partner with your child’s school.

Work with your child’s school more effectively when:

  • you and school staff have a clear, common understanding of your child
  • communication is open, factual and focuses on solutions
  • your child knows and asks for supports that work for them.

Build a shared understanding

When approaching the school, aim to develop a shared understanding of your child. This helps everyone to work towards the same goals.

  • Share your observations and/or your child’s perspective. This can be about a concern, or about a support that may be helpful.
  • Invite the school to provide their insights.
  • For example, “I’ve noticed that she is becoming increasingly upset about going to school in the last two months. She has said a few times now that she finds the classroom and shared areas too noisy, and she also seems to be having some friendship issues. Is there anything you’ve noticed at school that might be contributing to her reluctance to attend?”.
  • “I’ve noticed that he gets along really well with X, and seems more confident when they are together. I’m wondering if there might be ways that friendship could be used to better support him at school?”.

Use neutral language

When advocating, draw from examples that support the school’s understanding of the concern or request. This might be stating facts, focusing on solutions and avoiding emotive language during meetings.

  • For example, “I often feel that you aren’t understanding what my child needs at school” doesn’t support understanding and partnership. In comparison, “My child has shared that when they ask for a break due to feeling overwhelmed, they’re told to continue working. Can you tell me more about how break access is currently being managed?”
  • Use “I” statements, rather than “you” statements. And phrases that invite reflection (e.g. “I wonder…”, “Could you tell me more about …”). These can soften difficult questions.

Children can learn to self-advocate

Support your child’s opportunities to advocate at home and school.

  • Involve your child in decision making from an early age. For example, letting them suggest or choose strategies that can support them at home or school, and ‘how’ they feel most comfortable with seeking support on challenging days at school, helps them to develop their understanding of what works for them, and their ability to request these.

  • For secondary aged students, where possible, allow them to take a lead role in advocacy and decisions, rather than making decisions on their behalf.
